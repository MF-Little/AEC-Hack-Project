# Gaze Decoder: Session Process

How to run a capture session so that what we record is what the client genuinely responds to: not what they think we want to hear, not what happened to be brightest, and not what the designer steered them toward.

Draft for the team, 14 Nov 2026. The session is mostly unstructured: the client drives, the designer follows, and meaning is made afterwards. The Prototype Report already covers the measurement fixes (one trait at a time, pausing on idle, a random start, consent and a debrief). This document covers the human side.

## How a session runs

The client drives: where they go, what they look at, what they talk about, and when they want to see something different. The designer follows. If the client doesn't want to lead, the designer draws them out (see When the client isn't driving), then hands the lead back as soon as the client takes it.

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| Before | Consent. Set the camera to the client's eye height. Give them a clip-on mic. A minute in a practice room, only if they're new to the controls. |
| The walk | Most of the session, about 15–25 minutes, with no fixed agenda. The client moves, looks and talks; the designer works to the principles below. New options or styles come in when the client asks, or when the designer offers "want to see it another way?" at a natural pause. |
| Close | One light question: "If you could keep one thing from today, what would it be?" Cost and trade-offs, if needed, come after this. Optionally, show them what was recorded. |
| After | Team only. Decode the walk, separate first reactions from prompted ones, and name the session's mode (principle 5). This is where meaning is made. |

## The designer's principles

1. **Follow the client's lead.** They choose where to go, what to look at, what to talk about and when to switch options. Don't steer the view, point things out, or line up the next thing you want them to see.
2. **Always ask for more, without directing.** "Say more." "What else?" "And?" Or repeat their own word back: "Airy?" A useful test: a follow-up should contain only words the client has already used, or none. If your question brings in a new noun ("what about the frame?"), you're directing.
3. **Don't judge the response; stay neutral.** No "great!", no visible pleasure or disappointment, and no defending or explaining the design when they criticise it. That last one is the hardest when the designer runs the session, and it matters most: one defence teaches the client to stop criticising. If they ask what you think, turn it back: "I'm more curious what you see."
4. **Make meaning after the session, not during it.** Don't summarise or interpret back to them ("so you like curves?"). An interpretation said aloud becomes a suggestion they'll agree with. Keep your eyes off the live Observer panel, too; a teammate can watch it silently.
5. **Stay in the client's mode.** If the conversation is creative, keep it creative. If it's about safety, keep it about safety. Don't pull a dreamer into practicalities or a cautious client into blue-sky ideas. Park topics from another mode (cost, maintenance, code) until after the walk. The mode they choose is itself part of the vibe, so note it.
6. **Protect first reactions.** The first thing a client says about a space or an option is likely the most honest: it comes before they reason about it or adjust to you. Never talk over it, and wait until they've finished before asking anything. Small sounds count too ("oh", "hm", a laugh); note them if speech recognition drops them.

Three more that follow from these:

7. **Let silence work.** Count to five before speaking. People often add the real answer after a pause.
8. **When you must lead, lead where they go, not what they think.** "Shall we look over there?" is fine. "Isn't the light nicer over there?" is not.
9. **Mark it when you lead.** Anything the client says or looks at right after a prompt is partly yours. Mark the moment (a key press, once the app has one) so it's analysed separately from what they did on their own.

### Modes to listen for

| Mode | Sounds like | Stay in it by |
|---|---|---|
| Creative, aspirational | "What if…", "I've always wanted…", imagining | Following the images they use; "what else could happen here?" |
| Practical, everyday | "Where does the…", routines, storage, cleaning | Following the routine; "walk me through a normal day here" |
| Safety, risk | "Is it safe…", worries about children, slips, glare, security | Asking about each concern ("what worries you about it?") rather than reassuring or solving it on the spot |
| Emotional, memory | "This reminds me of…", stories | Letting the story finish; "tell me about that place" |
| Value, cost | "Is this expensive?", "is it worth it?" | Following it in their words ("worth it how?"); keeping actual prices for the end so they don't anchor the walk |

Psychologists describe the creative-versus-safety split as promotion focus versus prevention focus (Higgins 1997). People value an option more when its framing fits their focus (Higgins 2000), which is why switching mode mid-conversation changes the answers.

## When the client isn't driving

Some clients wait to be led. Start with the least directive move, go further only if needed, and hand the lead back as soon as they take it.

1. **Hand them the controls.** "Where would you like to go?" or "What's catching your eye?"
2. **Give them a situation.** "Picture a Saturday morning here. Where are you?"
3. **Offer a direction, not a target.** "Want to look closer, or step back and take in the whole room?"
4. **Offer a change.** "Want to see it another way?" Then show the next option or style without describing it.
5. **Last resort: an area, not an opinion.** "Let's stand by that wall for a moment." Don't say what's interesting about it.

Mark each of these as a prompt (principle 9).

## Biases and what we do about them

The Prototype Report already covers bundled traits, idle time counting as gaze, the start position, uneven sunlight and covert tracking. This list covers the rest. Because the client drives, some effects can't be prevented during the session, so we correct for them when decoding.

| Bias | In a walk it looks like | What we do |
|---|---|---|
| Courtesy | The client praises everything, especially to the designer's face | Never defend the design; say up front that criticism helps; now and then ask "what doesn't work here?" in their own mode |
| Being watched | They perform: aim the crosshair at windows, narrate for our benefit | Subject mode; don't explain the metrics; reveal in the debrief |
| Labels | "Japanese" or "low-iron, 91%" sets expectations before they look | Neutral labels; hide glass names and VLT in Subject mode; reveal after |
| Loudest voice | The senior person speaks first and everyone agrees | One person per session; group discussion after |
| The designer's thread | The designer's interests, or the live readout, quietly set the agenda | Principles 1, 4 and 9; a teammate watches the Observer view |
| Order and exposure | The client sees one option first, or for much longer, and it wins | Log the order and time per option; report gaze as a share of time on screen; compare first reactions across options |
| Contrast and light adaptation | After the bronze tint, clear glass looks dazzling; after clear, bronze looks gloomy | A short fade between options; read each option's first reaction with the previous one in mind |
| Brightness pulls the eye | Clear glass is brighter, so it gets more looks whether they like it or not. The glass taste test is affected today: it scores each glass by looks at the windows while it was in place. | For glass, weight what they said and the closing answer over gaze; compare returns and revisits rather than raw seconds |
| Looking where you walk | Whatever is ahead while walking piles up gaze | Count gaze while standing or turning; discount it while moving |
| Novelty and controls | The first minutes go on working out the keys | Practice room for newcomers; treat the first minute as a warm-up |
| Fatigue | Answers get shorter after 20–30 minutes | Let them end it; don't stretch the walk |
| Memory | The end-of-session verdict is driven by the best moment and the last one | Rely on reactions captured in the moment; treat the closing answer as one input |
| Anchors and decoys | A price, a "favourite" or an obviously weak option shifts the choice | No prices or designer favourites during the walk; only plausible options |
| Unequal render quality | The more detailed or better-lit option wins | Same model detail, materials, lighting and camera for every option |
| Eye height | The camera sits at 1.6 m; a 1,000 mm sill reads differently to someone whose eyes are at 1.9 m, or who is sitting | Set the eye height per person; let them sit if the room is for sitting |
| Ours | We read the data the way we hoped | Fix the decode rules before the sessions; one person decodes without knowing which option the team favours |

## When you need a fair comparison

A client-led walk is the right way to learn what one client wants. It's the wrong tool for "which of three glazing options do 30 staff prefer?", like the pilot in the AEC Opportunities doc. For that, add a short structured block after the walk, or run it as a separate study:

- Same spot, one trait changed per option.
- Each option for about 60 seconds, with a 3 s fade to grey between them.
- Rotate the order across people. Three options have six orders (ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA); use each equally often and log it.
- Show the first option again at the end (A B C A). If the reaction to A changes a lot, treat that comparison as low confidence.
- End with "which would you choose, and why?"

## Making it feel genuine

The more the walk feels like a test, the more people act like test-takers. Aim for a showroom visit with a good host.

- **Call it a walk-through, not a test.** "We'd like to show you some ideas" beats "we're going to measure your reactions".
- **Their pace.** No timer on screen, no rushing. Let them get lost, and don't correct their route.
- **Private.** Just the client and the designer. If a teammate observes, they sit out of view with the Observer screen.
- **A real situation.** Their own scenarios (their morning, their staff, their visitors) and real furniture, so they react to living there rather than judging a model.
- **An off switch.** "If you want to say something off the record, tell me and I'll pause the recording."
- **Close the loop.** Show them what we learned from their walk.

## Making meaning afterwards

The session collects; the team interprets. After each walk:

1. **Decode.** Run Decode walk and go through the notes and the timeline (and the replay, once the prototype has one). Don't share a reading with the client before this.
2. **First reactions first.** Pull out the first remark about each space and each option; they carry the most weight. Compare them across options, because the brightest or most unusual thing tends to get the first comment.
3. **Spontaneous vs prompted.** Separate what the client did and said on their own from what followed a designer prompt.
4. **Name the mode.** Creative, practical, safety, emotional or value: which did they keep coming back to? That's part of the vibe, and it shapes what to propose next.
5. **Three channels.** Each preference call shows what supports it: looked (gaze, revisits, approach), said (remarks and tone), and the closing answer. Two of three is medium confidence; all three is high.
6. **A second walk for big decisions.** For expensive, hard-to-reverse choices, run a short second walk a few days later. A preference that survives a few days is worth putting in the spec.
7. **Record the context.** Protocol version, designer, option order, device (desktop or headset), interruptions, and which moments were prompted.
8. **Keep "noticed" and "liked" separate** in every report, as the AEC Opportunities doc says.

## Consent and trust

- **What we say up front.** We record where you move, which way you're facing and what you say (as text, not audio), who will see it, and that you can pause, stop or ask us to delete it at any time.
- **What we keep until the debrief.** Which measures we compute and what we expect to find. This is so we don't steer them, not to hide anything; the debrief shows everything.
- **No upselling.** The data serves the client's decision. If a manufacturer is involved, say so.
- **Codes, not names.** Participant codes only, and a client lead doesn't get named results for their own staff.

## Designer script

**Welcome.** "Thanks for doing this. This is your walk: go wherever you like and say whatever comes to mind. Nothing is final, and telling us what doesn't work is just as useful as what does. I'll mostly follow you."

**Consent.** "While you walk, the app records where you go, which way you're facing and what you say, as text. Only the project team sees it, and you can pause or stop at any time."

**Following.** "Say more." "What else?" "And?" Or their own word back: "Airy?"

**If they ask what you think.** "I'm more curious what you see."

**If they stall.** "Where would you like to go?" Then: "Picture a Saturday morning here. Where are you?" Then: "Want to see it another way?"

**Close.** "If you could keep one thing from today, what would it be?"

**Debrief (optional).** "Here's what the app recorded. Does this match how it felt? Is there anything it got wrong?"

## Research to lean on

Cited from the standard literature. I didn't reopen these papers for this draft, so check them before quoting them in the pitch.

- **Rogers 1945.** The non-directive interview: following the person's lead and reflecting their words back draws out attitudes that direct questions miss. The method behind principles 1–3.
- **Zajonc 1980.** Feelings about something often come before thinking about it ("preferences need no inferences"). Supports principle 6.
- **Lindgaard et al. 2006.** People judged a web page's visual appeal within a fraction of a second, and those first judgements largely held. Supports principle 6.
- **Higgins 1997, 2000.** Promotion focus (aspiration, growth) versus prevention focus (safety, security), and people valuing an option more when its framing fits their focus. Supports principle 5.
- **Nisbett & Wilson 1977.** People often can't report why they prefer something, yet give confident reasons. Supports principle 4, and the case for decoding behaviour.
- **Orne 1962.** People in studies try to work out what's expected of them and act on it.
- **Johansson et al. 2005.** In "choice blindness" studies, people defended choices they hadn't made.
- **Ericsson & Simon 1993.** Saying what you notice changes little; explaining why changes behaviour.
- **van Gog et al. 2005.** Replaying someone's own eye movements prompts fuller recall than asking them afterwards without it.
- **Shimojo et al. 2003.** Just before choosing, gaze drifts to the option that will be chosen, and extra looking can raise liking.
- **Krajbich, Armel & Rangel 2010.** Options looked at longer are more likely to be chosen.
- **Zajonc 1968.** Repeated exposure increases liking.
- **Itti & Koch 2001.** Bright, high-contrast regions draw the eye regardless of meaning.
- **Kahneman et al. 1993.** Memory of an experience is driven by its peak and its end.
- **Huber, Payne & Puto 1982.** Adding a clearly weaker option shifts the choice between the others (the decoy effect).
- **Asch 1956.** People conform to a group's stated view.
